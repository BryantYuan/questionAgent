Key facts from those sources

Economics / feasibility
- No country has ever commercially mined Antarctica — not even before the ban.
- A metallic deposit would likely need to be "world class" in size or grade, with an in-place value of $200–$400 per ton, to be viable — and that depends on location.
- Any oil field would need at least several billion barrels of recoverable oil, and even then world oil prices would have to roughly double for it to be economically developable (OTA report, older figures but the scale of the point stands).
- On coal: an export market for Antarctic coal is judged "highly unlikely … in the foreseeable future," partly because recoverable reserves elsewhere are vast (China, US, Russia are the big producers).
- The MDPI valuation paper estimates Antarctica's realised economic value at ~2.46 trillion CNY, of which regulating services (climate, carbon, etc.) make up ~70%, and notes there is currently no value counted for mineral or freshwater resources because the Treaty restricts them.
- It also models a hypothetical potential total value (~105.62 trillion CNY) if resources were developed "at foreseeable scientific and technological levels" — i.e. a large theoretical number, contingent on technology and access.
- One science review notes that no economic deposits have been discovered in Antarctica, and that rising drilling/mining technology (plus the Arctic as a proof of concept) is increasing the risk that this could change.

Law / politics
- The Madrid Protocol (signed 1991, in force 1998) designates Antarctica as a "natural reserve, dedicated to peace and science."
- Article 7 expressly bans all activity related to mineral resource exploitation, except for scientific research.
- The ban is formally indefinite, but Article 25 allows a Consultative Party to request a review once 50 years have elapsed (i.e. around 2048); the Protocol also has withdrawal provisions.
- It replaced an earlier attempt (CRAMRA) that collapsed because parties couldn't agree on a mining regime — historical evidence that agreement on extraction is politically hard.
- The krill case shows that some resource use is already permitted and managed: CCAMLR governs it, and ~500,000 tonnes were taken in 2023/24 — but management consensus is fracturing, and a precautionary framework expired.

Environment
- Article 7's logic is to protect "dependent and associated ecosystems"; the Protocol explicitly affirms the "intrinsic value of Antarctica" — value independent of human use.
- Krill is described as a keystone species: "if krill goes … everything that eats them dies." Harvesting is contested — Greenpeace vs. CCAMLR's "no disruption" position vs. NGOs arguing what matters is when and where, not just how much.
- Environmental risks cited for mining generally: spills, habitat destruction, infrastructure (roads, runways, power) already required even for science.

Science / non-extractive value
- The MDPI paper's headline finding: the currently realised value of Antarctica is dominated by regulating and ecosystem services, not by material extraction.
- The Madrid Protocol frames science as the primary sanctioned activity.

Thinking scaffold — the boxes to think inside of

Box 1: "Worth it" — to whom, and measured how?
Facts to weigh: the protocol's own framing (peace + science + intrinsic value) vs. the valuation paper's split between regulating services (~70% of realised value) and material extraction (~0.4%). Ask yourself: are you pricing this by market profit, by global-commons value, or by scientific value? The answer changes depending on which.

Box 2: Economic feasibility
Facts: $200–400/ton in-place threshold; several-billion-barrel oil minimum; prices would need to roughly double; no deposits yet found; coal market "highly unlikely." Counter-facts: improving technology, Arctic precedent, and the hypothetical ~105 trillion CNY potential figure.

Box 3: Legal and political barriers
Facts: Article 7 ban; "indefinite" but Article 25 review around 2048; withdrawal provisions; CRAMRA's historical failure. What does "worth it" mean if it requires renegotiating a 50+ nation treaty?

Box 4: Environmental cost
Facts: keystone-species risk (the krill precedent); ecosystem protection as the Protocol's stated purpose; spill/habitat/infrastructure risks; the "intrinsic value" principle vs. a utilitarian cost-benefit lens.

Box 5: Geopolitics and precedent
Facts: 50+ consultative parties; the Arctic as a feasibility model; the argument that a ban becomes politically brittle once technology makes extraction profitable. Whose interests would a "yes" serve, and whose a "no"?

Box 6: The timing angle
Facts: 2048 review clause; 2024 expiry of part of krill management; rising catches. Is the honest question "worth it now" or "worth it eventually"?

Box 7: The evidence-quality check
Ask yourself: does the evidence I have actually agree? The old OTA figures are dated; the MDPI valuation is a modelling exercise; the science review says deposits are undiscovered. A defensible answer will note where the numbers are solid vs. speculative — including whether "worth it" can even be answered without knowing what's actually down there.

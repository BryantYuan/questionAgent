Is developing resources in Antarctica worth it?

MARKING CRITERIA

No supplied rubric was provided, so I selected the fixed OPINION markscheme. This fits because the question "Is developing resources in Antarctica worth it?" is a subjective/evaluative question with no single correct stance — it asks for a judgment ("worth it"), not a set of facts or a comparison. The OPINION criteria are: (1) Clear position, (2) Reasoning, not just assertion, (3) Addresses a counterpoint, (4) Internal consistency.

CRITERION-BY-CRITERION ASSESSMENT

  1. Clear position — MEETS.
    The student states a position unambiguously and repeats it in the conclusion: "I think developing resources in Antarctica is worth it" and "Overall I think we should develop Antarctica." There is no fence-sitting.

  2. Reasoning, not just assertion — PARTIALLY MEETS.
    The student does attach reasons to the thesis ("because there's a lot of untapped stuff there like oil and minerals," "The world is running out of resources"). However, these reasons are asserted rather than supported — no evidence is offered for the claim that the world is "running out of resources," and the economic benefit is stated in the vaguest terms ("companies could make a lot of money... which would help the economy"). More seriously, the one piece of reasoning used to dismiss the environmental objection — "Antarctica is already really cold and empty so nothing really lives there anyway" — is factually false. A check of the Wildlife of Antarctica reference confirms eight penguin species, seven pinniped (seal) species, ten cetaceans, dense Antarctic krill swarms, benthic communities, plus mosses, lichens, fungi and algae — with the Southern Ocean functioning as a major food web. So the reasoning is thin and one of its load-bearing claims is wrong, not merely underdeveloped.

  3. Addresses a counterpoint — MEETS (narrowly), but the treatment is flawed.
    The answer does acknowledge the opposing view: "Some people say it could damage the environment." So it is not ignoring the other side, which is what the criterion literally requires. But the way it engages is a strawman dismissal rather than a genuine reckoning with the objection — it waves the concern away with the false "nothing really lives there anyway" claim rather than weighing it. It clears the bar of "acknowledges an objection," but only just, and the dismissal misrepresents the size of the actual stakes.

  4. Internal consistency — MEETS.
    The conclusion does follow from the stated premises: the student asserts there are resources, asserts the world needs them, asserts money matters, and concludes development is worth it. The argument does not contradict itself. The soundness problem noted above (a false premise) is an accuracy/reasoning issue, not an internal-consistency issue, so this criterion still holds.

OVERALL IMPRESSION

The answer commits to a clear position and keeps its argument internally consistent, so it satisfies two of the four criteria outright. Where it falls short is depth and rigor: the supporting reasons are asserted without evidence, and the single counterargument the student raises is dismissed using a factual claim that is simply untrue — that "nothing really lives there anyway." The student also does not mention the standing legal reality that Antarctica's resources are currently off-limits (Article 7 of the Madrid Protocol prohibits mineral-resource activity other than scientific research), which is directly relevant context for any claim about "getting them from Antarctica." That omission is not required by the opinion rubric, but it shows the practical reasoning is built on assumptions that don't hold up. Overall the position is clear and consistent but under-reasoned, and its central rebuttal rests on an error rather than an argument.

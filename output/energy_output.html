Comparison of renewable and non-renewable energy sources

  • Renewable energy regenerates/replenishes faster than it is consumed, while non-renewable energy is finite and takes millions of years to form. [1][2][3]
  • Renewable sources include solar, wind, hydropower, geothermal, ocean, and bioenergy; non-renewable sources include coal, petroleum, oil, natural gas, and nuclear fuel/uranium. [1][2][3]
  • Renewables are described as sustainable and important for climate-change mitigation, while non-renewables are widely used, high in energy density, and linked to environmental concerns. [1]
  • Nuclear power is non-renewable but low-carbon, contributing about 10% of global electricity and roughly 6% of total world energy supply. [1]
  • Non-renewables produce far more CO₂: coal emits about 820 kg CO₂/MWh and natural gas about 350 kg, compared with solar and wind at about 10–50 kg including manufacturing. [2]
  • Renewables have higher upfront costs but low operating costs and falling prices, while non-renewables need continuous fuel purchases with volatile prices but provide consistent, weather-independent, high-density power. [2]
  • Renewable energy is considered cleaner and more environmentally friendly because it does not produce harmful pollutant emissions, whereas non-renewable energy produces harmful emissions and pollutants that can damage the environment. [3]

Setup and running costs of renewable and non-renewable energy sources

  • Most renewable energy costs are upfront construction/installation: natural gas plants about $1,000/kW, wind $1,200–1,700/kW, and residential solar $2,000–3,700/kW. [4]
  • Wind and solar farms often need new transmission lines and infrastructure because they are not sited near old non-renewable plants, adding setup costs. [4]
  • As of 2017, before tax credits, lifetime/levelized costs were wind $30–60/MWh, utility-scale solar $43–53/MWh, natural gas $42–78/MWh, and coal at least $60/MWh. [4]
  • Solar PV panels became over 80% cheaper since 2009, solar electricity costs fell almost 75% from 2010–2017, and wind turbine prices roughly halved over a similar period. [4]
  • The Royal Society estimated that a 2050 electricity system using only wind and solar power and 'green' hydrogen to reliably meet demand would have system costs of £56–£100/MWh. [5]
  • The OBR added BEIS's additional system costs to the levelised costs of gas and renewable energy sources to give an illustrative comparison of different energy sources' costs. [5]
  • The OBR's comparison uses fuel costs before and after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, which changed the relative costs of gas versus renewables for electricity generation. [5]

Environmental impact of renewable and non-renewable energy sources

  • The study compares the environmental effects of renewable and non-renewable energy across 20 countries using annual panel data from 1990 to 2022. [6]
  • It finds that renewable-energy countries have a much more positive environmental impact than countries relying on fossil fuels. [6]
  • The renewable countries' advantage is linked to better energy sources, modern environmental techniques, higher income, and better governance. [6]
  • Income-level shocks are positive in renewable countries but negative in non-renewable ones, so the study recommends improving income and energy-use levels in non-renewable countries to reduce pollution. [6]
  • Fossil fuels—coal, oil, and fossil gas—do substantially more environmental harm than renewable energy by most measures, including air and water pollution, public health, wildlife and habitat loss, water and land use, and heat-trapping emissions. [7]
  • Renewable energy sources also have significant environmental impacts, and their type and intensity vary by technology, location, and other factors. [7]
  • Wind and solar are clean in operation but can affect land use and wildlife/habitats; solar manufacturing can also involve hazardous materials. [7]
  • Biomass, hydropower, and geothermal have distinct impacts: biomass combustion can raise air/water concerns, hydropower impacts depend on dam/run-of-river [...]. [7]
  • Renewable energy generally produces far fewer greenhouse gases than fossil fuels like coal, oil, and natural gas, and renewable sources such as sunlight, wind, water, organic waste, and geothermal heat emit little to no greenhouse gases or air pollutants, with some producing no GHGs once operating. [8][9]
  • Renewable projects still have initial impacts from energy-intensive manufacturing of components and land clearing for installations. [8]
  • Ongoing impacts vary by technology: wind can harm birds/bats and disturb marine life, while hydropower dams can disrupt fish migration and reservoirs can emit GHGs. [8]
  • End-of-life renewable equipment can create hazardous waste, since solar panels may contain lead or cadmium and batteries may contain lithium or lead. [8]
  • Burning fossil fuels for energy, mainly electricity and heat, is the main source of greenhouse gases, and in 2023 the power sector was the largest source of global greenhouse gas emissions. [9]
  • Air pollution from burning fossil fuels—mainly fine particulate matter and nitrogen dioxide—is associated with 7 million premature deaths yearly and $8.1 trillion in annual health damage. [9]
  • Switching to clean energy like wind and solar helps address climate change as well as air pollution and human health. [9]

Reliability of renewable and non-renewable energy sources, including ability to provide power all the time

  • Renewable sources are intermittent because they depend on natural conditions: solar needs sunlight, wind needs sufficient wind, and hydro can be affected by drought or low water levels. [10]
  • Solar and wind are variable because their output depends on weather, time of day, and season; hydropower, geothermal, and biomass are firm because their output is reliably available on demand. [12]
  • Renewable storage is limited: batteries are expensive and short-lived, and pumped-storage hydro requires suitable terrain. [10]
  • Non-renewable sources are widely available and can supply power almost anywhere at any time, including continuously. [10]
  • Non-renewable fuels are more concentrated and efficient: coal and natural gas can reach about 40% and 60% efficiency, versus 15–20% for solar PV. [10]
  • No energy source can generate electricity 100% of the time; no power plant, whether renewable or fossil-fueled, is always available. [11][12]
  • Wind and solar, along with battery storage, can add flexibility and predictability that support a reliable grid. [11]
  • Renewables contribute to the electricity mix and can help keep power on cost-effectively. [11]
  • Grids can remain reliable with high shares of variable renewables by combining them with firm renewables, energy storage, and other low-carbon generating sources. [12]
  • When solar and wind are low, grids can use transmission over wide areas, storage, demand management, and flexible/backup generation such as geothermal, hydropower, nuclear, or fossil generation with carbon capture. [12]

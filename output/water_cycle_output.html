Main stages of the water cycle

  • The main stages of the water cycle are evaporation, transpiration, condensation, and precipitation. [2]
  • The most important processes in the water cycle are evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation, and runoff. [4]
  • Evaporation, sublimation, and transpiration move water into the atmosphere as vapor from surface water, ice, and plants. [1]
  • Evaporation happens when the sun heats water in oceans, lakes, and other bodies until it becomes gas and enters the air. [2]
  • Transpiration is similar: plant leaves release water vapor into the air. [2]
  • Condensation cools water vapor at high altitudes into tiny water droplets or ice crystals (or turns it back into liquid), forming clouds and fog. [1][2]
  • Precipitation occurs when clouds become too heavy and water falls, restarting the cycle. [2]
  • Precipitation returns condensed water to Earth as rain, snow, drizzle, sleet, or hail. [1]
  • Runoff carries water over the surface into rivers, lakes, seas, and oceans, while infiltration soaks it into soil and groundwater. [1]
  • The water cycle is commonly taught as a simple circular cycle of evaporation, condensation, and precipitation. [3]
  • A fuller description includes runoff from land and mountains into rivers, infiltration and percolation into soil, and groundwater flow; infiltration and groundwater flow are also shown as part of the cycle. [3][4]
  • It also includes plant uptake and transpiration, sublimation from ice and snow, and deposition. [3]

Why the water cycle matters for life on Earth

  • The water cycle is essential to all life on Earth; without it, life would not exist. [5]
  • All life on Earth needs water; water is vital for life and plays a role in everything from cellular function to climate. [6][8]
  • The water cycle enables water availability for all living organisms. [7]
  • The water cycle changes water between liquid, gas, and solid states. [6]
  • Without the water cycle, Earth would not have any fresh water. [6]
  • Without natural water recycling, clean water would run out, and clean water is essential to life. [7]
  • It regulates climate, supports ecosystems, enables human civilization, and shapes landscapes. [5]
  • It regulates weather patterns on Earth. [7]
  • The water cycle moves water through oceans, atmosphere, land, groundwater, and living things, helping make water available for ecosystems and human activities like agriculture and manufacturing. [8]
  • Evaporation absorbs heat and cools Earth's surface, while condensation releases heat, so the water cycle helps regulate climate. [8]
  • Water vapor traps the sun's heat and creates a greenhouse effect that warms the planet; without it, Earth would be a ball of ice. [8]
  • Water's ability to exist as solid, liquid, or gas lets the water cycle function as the engine that powers all life. [5]

Real example of the water cycle being disrupted, e.g. drought

  • Drought-dried land absorbs rainwater poorly, so heavy rain after a drought can run off and cause flash floods. [9]
  • Warmer temperatures produce more rain than snow; less snowpack means less meltwater during hot periods. [9]
  • Large-scale agricultural groundwater pumping is rapidly depleting aquifers that had stored water for thousands of years. [9]
  • Rivers diverted for agriculture can simply dry up, described as the most visible sign of a disrupted water cycle. [9]
  • Persistent drought dropped the Mississippi River to historic lows in 2023, allowing saltwater from the Gulf of Mexico to push upstream and contaminate urban drinking water supplies. [10]
  • Drought in Hawaii dried out vegetation and worsened wildfire risk, helping set the stage for the August 2023 Lahaina wildfire that killed 101 people. [10]
  • Drought across the Midwest and South from spring to fall became the most costly natural disaster of 2023, at $14.5 billion. [10]
  • California's 2023 atmospheric-river storms and heavy snow relieved drought but barely dented its groundwater deficit; groundwater levels remained lower than after the previous four-year drought ended in 2016. [10]
  • In 2024, severe drought hit the Amazon Basin and other parts of South America, as well as southern Africa. [11]
  • Brazil faced simultaneous extremes: catastrophic flooding in the south killed 183 people, while drought in the Amazon continued and affected 59% of Brazil's territory. [11]
  • Only about one-third of global river basins had normal conditions in 2024; the rest had too much or too little water, the sixth consecutive year of imbalance. [11]
  • Persistent groundwater deficits continued in parts of Africa, the Americas, and Australia, with only 38% of monitored wells at normal levels. [11]
